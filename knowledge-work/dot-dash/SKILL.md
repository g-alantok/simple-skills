name: dot-dash
description: Builds or checks a storyline as an SPQA pyramid of dots and dashes, so a decision-maker can follow it and act.
---

## Objective

Agree a storyline, built as an SPQA pyramid, that moves a decision-maker from what they already accept to the response the user wants. Check each dot, each change and the finished storyline against this: does each dot make the reader want the next one, and would the audience act for reasons that hold up?

Think as a partner at a top-tier management consultancy (McKinsey, BCG or Bain) would when storylining for a client. That means working out the answer from the evidence and then presenting it from the top down, and judging the storyline by the client's objectives or the objective of the user.

The storyline is the argument. Slides, reports and briefings are only how it is packaged. Get it right in text first, because moving a dot takes seconds and moving a slide takes much longer.

## Terms

- **Governing thought:** the one sentence the audience must take away, usually the decision or action wanted. In a deck it becomes the bottom line on slide 1.
- **Key line:** one of the three to five reasons or steps directly under the governing thought. In a deck, a section or a core slide.
- **Dot:** one claim, written as a full sentence that states the point. In a deck, one dot becomes one slide's lead line (action title).
- **Dash:** the evidence or reasoning under a dot, with its source. In a deck, the slide content.
- **Pyramid:** the governing thought sits on top, and each layer below supports the one above (Minto's Pyramid Principle).
- **SPQA:** Minto's situation, complication, question, answer, with the complication framed as the problem.
- **Horizontal logic:** the dots, read alone and in order, tell the whole story.
- **Vertical logic:** each dot's dashes prove it at the strength it claims.
- **MECE:** mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Points grouped under one point don't overlap, and together they cover everything it claims.

## Input

Start once you know the audience, the decision intent and the governing thought. Decision intent is what the audience must do: approve, choose, act, align or escalate. Ask only for what's missing, one short question at a time, with your best guess attached so the user can agree or correct it. If the user brings an existing storyline or deck, write it back as dots and dashes before changing anything. If the problem itself isn't solved yet, suggest breaking it down first (with a structured-thinking skill if the user has one), because a storyline can't fix an unsolved problem.

Open references/storyline-patterns.md when choosing the order, when SPQA fits badly, when the deck runs under six or over 20 slides, or when the user wants a worked example.

## Method

1. **Frame the story with SPQA.** Situation: what the audience already agrees with. Problem: what has changed to make that untenable, which is the reason to act now. Question: the one the audience now asks. Answer: the governing thought. Choose the order from the decision intent: answer first when the audience expects a recommendation or has little time (approve, escalate), build to the answer when they are sceptical or must weigh options (choose), and diagnosis first when they don't yet agree there is a problem (align, and sometimes act). Choose the emphasis from what this audience responds to: the opportunity (what they gain) or the risk (what they lose by waiting). Give the reason for both in one line. Keep the situation and problem short, because most of the storyline belongs to the answer.
2. **Draft the pyramid.** Under the governing thought, write three to five MECE key lines that each answer "why?" or "how?". Decide how each group reasons. Inductive means separate pieces of evidence that each support the point, which is safer because one can fall and the rest still stand. Deductive means a chain where each step relies on the last, which is strong but breaks if any step falls. Order each group by importance, by time, or by a structure the audience already uses, such as their business units or stated priorities. Under each key line write the dots, and under each dot two to four dashes. Write each dot as one full sentence of about 15 words or fewer. Keep this draft to yourself until steps 3 to 5 are done.
3. **Check the bottom line is up front.** Do this as its own pass once the draft is complete. You write one word at a time, so a first draft tends to reason through the background and reach the point last. Read each dot and move its point to the front. Then read the whole storyline, and in an answer-first story, move the governing thought and the decision to the top if they first appear near the end.
4. **Test the horizontal logic.** Read the dots alone, in order, as the audience would.
   - Name the link between each pair of dots (because, so, but, then) and any unstated premise it needs. A linking word names a link without proving it.
   - Check that each key line answers the question the one before it raises. A dot that neither raises nor answers a question is a list item, so cut it or move it.
   - Trace every dot back to the governing thought, and move any dot that doesn't serve it to the appendix or cut it. Check that the first dot says why the audience should care and the last says what happens next.
   - Name the first thing a sceptical decision-maker would challenge, and check the storyline answers it. In a choose or approve story, check that doing nothing is shown as a baseline.
   - Check that terms, timeframes and scope stay the same throughout, and that no two dots make the same point.
5. **Test the vertical logic.** For each dot, check that its dashes are on point, strong enough for the claim, from a source you can name, and independent of each other. Where support is missing, write "Evidence needed:" and what would settle it. Where a dash supports a different dot, move it there.
6. **Stop and ask.** Show the storyline, then the two or three breaks that matter most, each naming the dot, the gap and a fix. Ask: "Fix it, qualify the claim, or park it?" Apply only what the user agrees to, then rerun steps 3 to 5 on the dots that changed.

## Guidelines and verification

| Instead of | Do |
|---|---|
| A topic label as a dot ("Market context") | Write a full sentence that states the point |
| Following the order the analysis was done in | Order by what the audience needs to hear to reach the decision |
| Smoothing a gap with a linking word | Name the missing premise and ask |
| Treating the user's claim as proven | Label it "Proposed claim" until a dash supports it |
| Reporting every check | Report only the breaks that change the argument |
| Designing slides or their layout | Stop at the storyline and hand over to executive-ready-slides |
| Re-diagnosing the whole problem | Turn a big gap into one question for the user |

Before reporting a break, quote the dot it rests on. Before calling a dot supported, point to the dash that supports it.

## Output

The storyline in chat, in this shape:

<example>

> **Audience:** University compliance committee
> **Decision:** Approve a three-month AI pilot
> **Order:** Answer first, risk emphasis, because this committee approves change once the risk is contained
> **S:** The central team files the annual return on time every year.
> **P:** It now takes six weeks, and that time grows each year.
> **Q:** How do we cut the time without putting the return at risk?
> **A (governing thought):** Approve a three-month pilot in one team to test whether AI can halve the time.
>
> **1. Most of the six weeks goes on chasing and checking, which AI handles well.**
> **1.1** Chasing data owners takes about half the time.
> - Evidence needed: a time breakdown by task
> **1.2** Checking the return against last year's figures follows fixed rules.
> - Source: team process map, 2025
>
> **2. A pilot in one team keeps the risk small.**
> **2.1** The current process runs alongside the pilot, so the return is never at risk.
>
> **3. Three months covers one full cycle, so the committee will have a clear result by June.**

</example>

Keep each dot's number when dots move, and give new dots new numbers, so changes can be traced into the deck. If the user asks to save the storyline, save it as dot-dash.md in the same shape. When the user is ready for slides, hand over like this: the governing thought becomes the bottom line, key lines become sections or executive summary points, dots become lead lines, dashes become slide content, and "Evidence needed" becomes "to confirm".

## Done when

The user has agreed the SPQA frame, the order and the pyramid. In an answer-first story the governing thought comes first, every dot is a full sentence with support or an "Evidence needed" label, and the dots alone tell a story a sceptical decision-maker would act on.
