# Storyline patterns

Open this file when choosing the order, when SPQA fits badly, when the deck runs under six or over 20 slides, or when the user wants a worked example.

## 1. Decision intent sets the order

| Intent | Default order | Why | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Approve | Answer first | They expect a recommendation, so give it and then prove it | A sceptical audience resists before you have earned the right to recommend |
| Choose | Build to the answer, or options and decision | They need to see the options weighed fairly before you steer | Taking so long that they lose patience |
| Act | Before, after, bridge, or diagnosis then answer | They need to feel the gap between today and what is possible | Selling the future before they accept today's problem |
| Align | Diagnosis first | The answer is a shared view of the problem, not yet a recommendation | An audience expecting a recommendation feels let down, so say up front what the session is for |
| Escalate | Answer first, with severity | Bad news held back reads as hiding it | Softening the problem until nobody acts |

These are defaults. The deciding question is always: what does this audience need to hear first to stay engaged and reach the right conclusion?

## 2. When SPQA fits badly

SPQA fits most consulting stories. The shapes below still contain a situation, a problem and an answer, and they shift where attention goes.

- **Before, after, bridge:** where we are (painful), where we could be, and the plan to get there. Use for transformation and change programmes.
- **Options and decision:** context, the criteria, each option scored against them (always including doing nothing), then the recommendation. Use when the options are the story, such as vendor selection or a strategy fork.
- **Problem, solution, why now, proof, ask:** the pitch shape from venture capital. Use when selling a vision to a board, sponsor or investor. The problem leads because people fund big problems, not clever solutions.
- **Cascade:** the governing thought splits into three to five sections, and each is its own small SPQA. Use for board packs, strategy documents and readouts across several workstreams.

## 3. Deck length

- **Micro (3 to 5 slides):** answer, proof, proof, next steps. Leave out situation and agenda slides, show the single strongest chart for each point, and move context to the spoken introduction or a pre-read.
- **Standard (8 to 15 slides):** front page, executive summary, situation and problem (one or two slides together), diagnosis (two to four), recommendation (one or two), risks and next steps (one or two), appendix. Spend at least 60% of the slides on the answer.
- **Long (20 or more slides):** front page, executive summary, then sections that each open with a divider stating the section's point and run three to five slides, followed by the combined recommendation, next steps and appendix. Test the horizontal logic twice: section dividers alone, then the lead lines inside each section.

Write the executive summary last and place it first. It states the governing thought and the key lines in three to five points. Move anything nice to know but not needed for the decision to the appendix.

## 4. The hierarchy and the strip test

Level 1 is the governing thought, level 2 the key lines, level 3 the dots, and level 4 the dashes (the data). Each level sums up the one below it.

Strip test: read one level on its own. The governing thought should stand alone, the key lines alone should tell the story, and the dots under each key line should prove it. If a level only makes sense once you read the level below, its claims are too vague.

## 5. Worked example: approval deck, answer first

Governing thought: We should invest $1.8M in automated claims triage to cut processing time by 60% and pay back within 14 months.

1. Claims take 14 days on average, well above the industry benchmark of five.
   - Time by stage: intake 3 days, assessment 6, decision 3, payment 2
   - Complaints about processing time up 40% on last year
2. Slow processing costs us $3.2M a year in rework, complaints and lost customers.
   - $1.4M rework from manual errors in assessment
   - 34% of departing customers name slow claims as the main reason (exit survey)
3. Assessment is the bottleneck, taking 6 of the 14 days and causing 70% of errors.
   - Process map showing where delays build up
   - Error share by stage: assessment 70%, intake 20%, other 10%
4. Automated triage cut assessment from six days to under one in a 200-claim pilot, at 95% accuracy.
   - Pilot results: 95.3% accuracy, 0.8 days average
5. Building with a partner is the fastest and lowest-risk of the three options.
   - Build, buy, and build with partner, scored on speed, cost, risk and flexibility
   - Doing nothing keeps the $3.2M annual cost
6. The $1.8M investment breaks even at month 14.
   - $1.2M build, $400K change management, $200K contingency
   - Break-even between months 12 and 18 across adoption scenarios
7. The three main risks each have a specific fix.
   - Staff resistance: early involvement and a change programme
   - Accuracy shortfall: 90 days of running both processes, with manual override
8. Approving today lets the programme start in Q2.
   - First gate review at week 8, steering committee monthly

Eight dots make the body. With a front page, executive summary and appendix, the deck runs to about 12 slides, and the dots alone tell the story. The figures are illustrative.
