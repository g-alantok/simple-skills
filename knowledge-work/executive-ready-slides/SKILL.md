## Objective

Help the user make a deck that a busy reader understands fast and is persuaded by: the headlines alone give the message and the ask, each slide's content proves its headline at a glance, and the headlines in order tell a story the reader wants to follow. Busy readers often read only the headlines, so a headline that only names a topic ("Objective", "Background") leaves them to guess the point, and a slide whose content argues something else loses their trust. Fast isn't enough on its own, because a deck of correct headlines with nothing at stake between them gives the reader no reason to act. Check every headline, every slide, every change and the finished deck against this: would the reader get the point and the ask, believe it, and see why it matters now?

## Terms

- **Lead line:** the headline of a slide, written as one full sentence that states the slide's point. Management consultants call this an action title. "Costs rose 20% because of overtime" is a lead line. "Costs" is a label.
- **Bottom line:** the one sentence the audience must take away, usually what you want them to decide or do.
- **Bottom line up front (BLUF):** the point comes first, then the reasons for it.
- **Horizontal logic:** the lead lines, read alone and in order, tell the whole story, each raising the question the next one answers. Minto's shape for the story is situation (where things stand), complication (what has changed or gone wrong) and resolution (what to do about it). Bottom line first doesn't kill the story: the first slide gives the resolution, and the slides after it give the situation and complication that make it matter.
- **Vertical logic:** on each slide, the content under the lead line proves that lead line.
- **Pyramid Principle:** Barbara Minto's structure for an argument. The bottom line sits at the top, the lead lines that give the reasons sit under it, and each slide's content is the evidence under its lead line. At every level, the points grouped under one point are MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive): no two overlap, and together they cover everything the point above them claims.

## Input

Start with one question: "Who is this deck for, what do you want them to do after seeing it, and what are you starting from: an idea or notes, research, a storyline, or a deck that already exists?" Use your best guess for anything still missing, and say what you assumed. The starting point changes how you work (see "Adjust for the starting point" under Method), but not the standard the deck must meet.

## Method

Use the Pyramid Principle and consulting practice for action titles and slide layout throughout. You write one word at a time, so your first draft tends to reason through the background and reach the point last. For that reason, draft first, check, rewrite, and show the user only the rewritten version.

### Adjust for the starting point

The steps below suit every deck. What changes is where the bottom line comes from and what tends to go wrong.

- **From scratch (an idea or notes):** the risk is a deck that sounds sure with nothing behind it. In step 2, propose the content for each slide from what they gave you, and mark any evidence they haven't supplied "to confirm".
- **From research (reports, data or interviews):** the risk is a deck that follows the order the research was done in, so it reads as a tour of findings with no point. In step 1, write the bottom line from what the findings show for this audience, and if the findings don't back the bottom line the user wants, say so before going on. In step 2, pick the evidence that proves each lead line, note its source, and list the findings you left out so the user can bring any back.
- **Auditing an existing deck:** the risk is rewriting a deck the user mostly likes. Before step 1, list its current lead lines and state the bottom line the deck makes now, so the user can compare it with the one they want. Skip step 2 and run steps 3 to 6 on their slides, fixing the order before the wording. At step 7, show findings rather than a rewritten deck: quote each current lead line next to your suggested one, and rewrite only what the user agrees to.
- **From a storyline (dots and dashes, or any outline the user has agreed):** the risk is redoing an argument that is already settled. In step 1, take its main point (the governing thought) as the bottom line and check it rather than rewrite it. In step 2, turn each dot into a lead line and its dashes into slide content, keep the dot numbers, and carry "Evidence needed" over as "to confirm".

### When to run a dot-dash check

A dot-dash strips a deck back to its lead lines (dots) and the evidence under each (dashes), so you can see the argument without the layout. Open references/dot-dash-check.md and run it:
- before step 2, when starting from notes or research and the deck asks for a decision or runs past five slides
- at the start of an audit, to see the argument the deck makes now
- when step 5 fails, because the lead lines read as a list of findings or step 3 had to move the bottom line
- when step 6 finds a slide whose content doesn't prove its lead line, or proves a different point
- after any reorder or change to the bottom line

If your tool can start a sub-agent, give it the bottom line, the lead lines, the slide content and references/dot-dash-check.md, but not the notes or this conversation, and ask it to run the check and report the breaks. A sub-agent starts with only what you hand it, so pass it the file or paste its text. A reader who hasn't seen the drafting can't fill a gap with what the author meant, so it catches gaps you would read past. Otherwise, run the check yourself.

Skip it for a deck of three slides or fewer. If the user brings an agreed storyline, run the check once instead of rebuilding it.

1. **Write the bottom line.** In one sentence, state what the audience should take away or do. Before showing it, check it against the objective: does the point come first, and would the audience know what is being asked? Rewrite it until both are true. Then show it and ask: "Is this the message, or should it change?" Agree it before drafting, because every slide has to serve it.
2. **Draft the slides.** Write one lead line per slide, with the content that supports it underneath. Keep this draft to yourself until steps 3 to 6 are done.
3. **Check for the bottom line up front.** Do this as a separate pass once the draft is complete, because now the whole draft is in front of you. Read each lead line, and where its point comes at the end, rewrite it so the point comes first. Then read the deck, and if the bottom line only appears near the end, move it to the first slide and let the slides after it give the reasons.
4. **Check each lead line against consulting standards.** Check that each one makes a claim the reader could agree or disagree with, is specific to this situation, uses numbers where the user has them, states a finding rather than an activity ("Costs rose 20%" rather than "We reviewed costs"), and fits in two lines. Rewrite any that fail.
5. **Check the horizontal logic against the objective.** Read the rewritten lead lines alone and in order, as the audience would. Check that each raises the question the next one answers, that the slides giving reasons for the bottom line are MECE (no two make the same point, and no question a sceptical reader would ask is left unanswered), and that the deck shows what is at stake if the reader does nothing. Leave the slides that set up the situation and complication out of the MECE check, because they frame the reasons rather than adding to them. Where they read as a list of separate findings, reorder them or add the complication that links them. Rewrite until a reader of the headlines alone would get the point and the ask and want to act, or note the gap that remains.
6. **Check the vertical logic and layout of each slide.** For each slide, check that the content proves the lead line at the strength it claims, that the points under the lead line are MECE, that the slide carries one message, and that the layout shows the point first: the strongest evidence nearest the lead line, a chart or table labelled with what it shows, the source noted, and only the text the point needs. Where the content leaves part of the lead line unproven, add the missing evidence or narrow the lead line. Where two points overlap, merge them, because repeats read as padding. Move content that supports a different point to the slide it belongs on.
7. **Stop and ask.** Show the rewritten deck, then the two or three problems that still need the user's call, each with a suggested fix. Ask: "Take these fixes, change them, or leave them?" Apply only what the user agrees to, then rerun steps 3 to 6 on the slides that changed before showing them again.

## Guidelines and verification

| Instead of | Do |
|---|---|
| A label as a headline ("Objective", "Next steps") | Write the full sentence the slide proves ("We need approval by June to start the pilot") |
| Building up to the point at the end | Put the point first and the reasons after it |
| A vague lead line ("Some considerations around costs") | State the claim, and mark it "to confirm" if the evidence isn't there yet |
| Lead lines that read as a list of separate findings | Order them so each raises the question the next one answers, and name what is at stake |
| Choosing fonts, colours or branding | Leave branding to the user's template, and arrange the content so the point is seen first |

Before calling a slide finished, quote its lead line and point to the content that proves it.

## Output

The deck in chat: each slide's lead line, then its content and layout in a few short lines, with the bottom line on the first slide, like this:

> **For:** Leadership team
> **Bottom line:** Fund a shared customer inbox so replies take one day instead of three.
>
> **1. A shared customer inbox would cut our reply time from three days to one, and we're asking you to fund it.**
> Three boxes: the cost, the time saved, the decision needed this month.
>
> **2. Replies take three days because questions arrive in five places and nobody owns them.**
> Bar chart of reply time by channel, slowest first, labelled "Average days to reply, Jan to Sep". Source: support log.
>
> **3. Two other teams halved their reply times within a quarter of moving to one inbox.**
> Before-and-after chart for both teams. To confirm: their figures.
>
> **4. It costs $12,000 a year, less than the overtime we now spend chasing replies.**
> Two-column comparison: inbox cost against current overtime.
>
> **5. A decision this month gets it running before the end-of-year peak.**
> Timeline from decision to go-live, with the peak marked.

Read together, the lead lines also work as the deck's executive summary.

For an audit, show the bottom line the deck makes now beside the agreed one, then quote each current lead line beside your suggested one with a one-line reason, and leave the slides unchanged until the user agrees.

## Done when

The user has agreed the bottom line and the deck (for an audit, which fixes stand), every lead line is a full sentence with its point first that meets the step 4 standards, the lead lines read alone give the message and the ask and tell a story with something at stake, and each slide's content proves its lead line.
