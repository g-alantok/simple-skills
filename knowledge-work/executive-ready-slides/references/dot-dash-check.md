# Dot-dash check

A dot-dash is the consulting way to plan and test a deck's argument in text before building slides. Dots are the lead lines and dashes are the evidence under each one. The skill says when to run this check, and this file says how.

## Why it works

In a short outline every dot is in view at once, so a gap, a repeat or a wrong order is easy to see. On slides, layout and detail compete for attention and the argument gets harder to follow. Moving a dot takes seconds, and moving a slide takes much longer.

## Build one

1. Write the bottom line as one full sentence: what the audience should take away or do.
2. Under it, write three to five dots. Each is a full sentence giving one reason or step that supports the bottom line. These become the lead lines of the main slides or sections.
3. Under each dot, write two to four dashes: the evidence or reasoning that proves it, each with its source. These become the slide content.
4. Mark any dash the user hasn't supplied "to confirm".

For an audit, write the deck's current lead lines as dots and its slide content as dashes, without changing them.

> **Bottom line:** Fund a shared customer inbox so replies take one day instead of three.
> - Replies take three days because questions arrive in five places and nobody owns them.
>   - Average days to reply by channel, Jan to Sep (support log)
>   - Three of the five channels have no named owner
> - Two other teams halved their reply times within a quarter of moving to one inbox.
>   - To confirm: their before-and-after figures
> - It costs $12,000 a year, less than the overtime we now spend chasing replies.
>   - Inbox cost against this year's overtime (finance report)

## Check the horizontal logic

Read the dots alone, in order, as the audience would.

- Name the link between each pair of dots (because, so, but, then). Where you can't name one, there is a gap: add the missing dot or reorder. A linking word in the text names a link without proving it.
- Check that each dot answers the question the one before it raises. A dot that does neither is a list item, so cut it, merge it or move it to the appendix.
- Ask "so what?" of each dot. If the answer doesn't lead back to the bottom line, the dot doesn't belong, because the dots must prove the bottom line, not just share its topic.
- Check that the dots giving reasons for the bottom line are MECE: no two make the same point, and no question a sceptical reader would ask is left unanswered. Leave dots that only set up the situation out of this check.
- Check that the first dot says why the audience should care, the last says what happens next, and the dots show what is at stake if nothing is done.

## Check the vertical logic

For each dot, check that its dashes:

- support this dot, not a neighbour (move a dash that belongs elsewhere)
- are strong enough for the claim (where they prove less, narrow the dot)
- have a source the user can name, or are marked "to confirm"

## Report

Report only the breaks that change the argument. Quote the dot, name the gap and suggest a fix. Then go back to the step of the skill that sent you here.
